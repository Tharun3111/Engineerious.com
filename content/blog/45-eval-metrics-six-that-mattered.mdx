The first eval suite I built for a production RAG assistant had 45 metrics in it. Faithfulness,
answer relevance, context precision, context recall, toxicity, latency percentiles, refusal rate,
citation coverage, a half-dozen LLM-as-judge rubrics, and a long tail of things I added because a
paper or a vendor dashboard suggested them.

It ran on every PR. It produced a beautiful table. And when we shipped a change that made the
assistant noticeably worse, the table barely moved.

That is the failure mode this post is about. Not "we had no evals" — we had too many, and no idea
which ones carried signal.

## The test: does the metric move when quality moves?

The question a metric has to answer is narrow. **When a human says this build is worse, does the
number go down?** Everything else is decoration.

So I built the cheapest possible version of that experiment:

1. Take 60 real production conversations, sampled across the traffic distribution — not the happy
   path, not only the escalations.
2. Generate answers from two builds: the current production model/prompt/retriever, and a build I
   had deliberately degraded in a specific way (a worse chunker, a smaller model, a truncated
   system prompt, a broken reranker).
3. Have a human — me, then a colleague, blind — label which answer is better, or tie.
4. Run all 45 metrics against both sides.
5. Ask, per metric: how often does it agree with the human ordering?

A metric that agrees 50% of the time on a binary comparison is a coin flip. It is not a weak
signal, it is *no* signal, and its presence in the report is actively harmful because it dilutes
the ones that work.

## What survived

Six metrics cleared the bar with enough margin that I trusted them to gate a deploy:

- **Retrieval recall@k against a labelled set.** Not judged, measured. If the passage containing
  the answer is not in the context, nothing downstream can save the generation. This one caught
  more real regressions than every generation-side metric combined.
- **Citation-support rate.** For each factual claim in the answer, is there a retrieved chunk that
  actually supports it? Judged, but with a tight rubric and a per-claim decomposition rather than a
  whole-answer score.
- **Refusal rate on answerable questions.** A model that gets more cautious looks safe in
  aggregate and is a disaster in practice. This moved sharply on every model swap.
- **Exact-match on the structured slice.** About a fifth of our traffic had a verifiable answer —
  an ID, a date, a status. No judge required, no ambiguity.
- **p95 end-to-end latency.** Quality that arrives after the user gave up is not quality.
- **Cost per resolved conversation.** Not per call. Per *resolved conversation*, which counts the
  retries a worse build provokes.

Note what those have in common: four of them are counts of a verifiable thing. Only one is a pure
LLM-as-judge rubric, and it only survived after I decomposed it from "rate this answer 1–5" into
"for each claim, supported or not".

## What died, and why

The casualties are more instructive than the survivors.

**Most 1–5 judge rubrics.** Ask a model to rate helpfulness on a five-point scale and you get a
distribution with almost all its mass on 4. Degrade the system meaningfully and you get a
distribution with almost all its mass on 4. The scale has no resolution where you need it. Binary
or per-claim judgments held up; Likert scales did not.

**Every metric that scored an answer without reference to the retrieved context.** They measure
whether the model writes fluently, which it always does now.

**Aggregate toxicity and safety scores on a non-adversarial set.** Zero variance across every
build I tested. That does not mean safety does not matter — it means this measurement was not
doing the work. Adversarial sets are a different eval with a different cadence.

**Anything averaged over the full traffic mix.** The mean hid everything. The regression I most
wanted to catch lived in 8% of queries, and an 8% subpopulation moving from good to terrible
changes a mean by a rounding error. Slicing before averaging was the single highest-leverage change
I made to the whole suite.

## The discipline, generalised

You do not need my six metrics. You need the procedure that produced them:

1. **Write down what a regression looks like** in your product, in a sentence, before you pick any
   metric.
2. **Manufacture that regression on purpose.** A deliberately broken build is the only ground truth
   you can generate on demand.
3. **Keep only metrics that separate the broken build from the good one**, on your data, at your
   sample size.
4. **Slice before you average.** Per intent, per document source, per conversation length —
   whichever dimension your product actually varies along.
5. **Re-run the selection quarterly.** Metric relevance decays as the system changes. The suite
   that catches your chunker bug will not catch your agent-loop bug.

## The part that is still unsolved

Sample size. Sixty conversations is enough to tell a coin flip from a signal; it is not enough to
resolve a 2% quality change, and most of the changes I ship now are 2% changes. Getting statistical
power without hand-labelling thousands of examples is the open problem in my own setup, and I do
not have a clean answer yet. Pairwise judging with a strong model and a human-audited subsample is
where I have landed — provisionally.

If you take one thing: an eval suite is not a dashboard, it is a **classifier for regressions**, and
you should evaluate it exactly as you would evaluate any other classifier. Most suites, mine
included, would not have passed their own test.
